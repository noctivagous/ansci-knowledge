The correspondence web, in chords and bundles

The schemes held in ansci-knowledge connect the five phases （五行） to seasons, flavours, organs, grains, animals, the creature classes of the Yueling, and — through the trigrams — to the sixty-four hexagrams. Drawn as chords and edge bundles, the same assertions show their shape at three distances: the flows between groups, one source's table at a time, and every individual value tied to its phase. At the centre of each circular diagram a taijitu gauge reads the yin–yang proportion of whatever is selected — for trigrams and hexagrams the proportion is exact, counted from their lines (yang lines over 3, resp. 6); for the phases the values are schematic — Fire at peak yang (~90%), Water at peak yin (~10% yang), Wood rising yang, Metal rising yin, Earth at balance (~50%) — and are labelled schematic wherever they appear. The readout also names the movement — waxing, waning, at a peak, balanced — and a setting in the tab bar can add the same state as a complex number (z = balance + i·momentum) with a phasor hand on the gauge. At rest the gauge shows the balance of the whole displayed system. Every trigram and hexagram glyph is drawn as vector lines generated from the figure's own line data — solid yang, broken yin, bottom line first — never as a font character, so the glyphs stay exact at any size; every figure also carries its English name beside the Chinese and pinyin. Each category on the circle sits inside a big outline ring-sector container that encloses its labels — the creature classes （鱗 scaled, 羽 feathered, 倮 naked, 毛 furred, 介 shelled) inside the creature-class container, and likewise the phases, flavours, seasons, trigrams and Luoshu positions; in the ring view, each upper trigram's hexagrams inside its sector container; in the composition view, the trigrams of each phase; in the bagua view, the octagon is sized the same way, enclosing each station's name and element labels — and hovering a container highlights its members. On every chart, when an item is highlighted — hovered or selected — a capsule (a pill outline, turned to the label's own angle around the circle) is drawn around its label and around the labels of the items it links to, so both ends of a highlighted relation can be found immediately. A fifth chart, the Wheel, shows another aspect of the same records: yin and yang divided by a diameter at the centre, and the five phases as a seasonal ring around it — Wood and Fire in the yang half, Earth astride the diameter, Metal and Water in the yin half — with each phase's season and Luoshu associations carried inside its own ring-sector. A sixth chart, the Bagua, stands alone: the eight trigrams in their octagon around a yin–yang centre, with no Wuxing ring beneath them. Each trigram's element is stated as its association — data, not geography — because the eight stations (45° apart) and the five sectors (72° apart) partition the circle differently and cannot be drawn as one shared geometry without the drawing starting to misstate the correspondences. Different charts show different aspects of the same phenomena; each is bound to the same records, so a thing selected in one aspect is the same thing in all the others.

1 · Flows among phases, flavours, seasons and creature classes

The five phases, the five flavours, the five seasons and the five creature classes （五蟲） — and, with the 洛書·八卦 layer on, the nine Luoshu positions and the eight trigrams join the same circle, so a position's ribbons to its trigram and its phase, and a trigram's ribbon to its phase, are drawn in the same view as the cycles. Flows B is the same rim, the same shells and the same records with a different centre: the five phases sit at the centre as a pentagon in their Wheel arrangement — Wood at the left, Fire at the top, Earth at the right-hand pivot, Metal and Water in the yin half — the generating cycle drawn as arrows around the pentagon and the controlling cycle as the star inside it, and every rim item draws a spoke inward to its own phase instead of a chord across the disc, so each phase's whole correspondence family reads at a glance and clicking a centre phase isolates it. Where a scheme keys an item to a second phase, the item draws a second, dashed spoke to that phase as well — the disagreement is the split. A ribbon runs from a node to what it produces, governs or is assigned to — the generating cycle （相生） and controlling cycle （相剋） among the phases themselves; the Hongfan's flavour production (each phase's process makes a flavour); Suwen ch. 4's phase–season assignments; the Yueling's season–class assignments; and the classes' phase membership as the Yueling commentary tradition states it. Ribbon width counts the kinds of link between two nodes. Turn link kinds off to read one system at a time. Click a ribbon or a node and the panel lists the assertions it aggregates — scheme, tier and source for each one; nothing in the diagram is only a count.

2 · The correspondence tables, one source at a time

Each source states its own table, and the tables do not all agree. All nine correspondence schemes of the repository are layers here — the two partial ones （淮南子·精神， a single passage; and the 月令's own attested cells) are marked partial, their missing cells left empty rather than completed from another source. The sources are layers: switch one off and every chord re-forms from the sources that remain. With several layers on, a chord's width counts the sources asserting that family for that phase, and a chord with a dark outline marks a cell where the sources disagree — the Shize animal row is transposed against Suwen ch. 4 (spring takes the goat, summer the chicken), and its grain row differs in three cells. Clicking any chord lists the exact assertions inside it, so a precise question — what does the Shize say about spring's animal? — is answered by the rows themselves, disagreements flagged and left unresolved.

3 · Every value, bundled to its phase

Hierarchical edge bundling draws each leaf on a circle, grouped by family, and routes its link through the hierarchy — links that share a path overlap into a bundle, so the weight of a whole family reads at a glance. Four link sets share the circle: the Suwen ch. 4 values tied to their phases; the Yueling creature classes with their members (attested members, and members the class criterion predicts — frog and spider have no assertion in the scheme and are absent, not filled in); and the eight trigrams tied to their phases in the Later Heaven arrangement, with each trigram also joined to its opposite in the King Wen pairing. Two trigrams, one above the other, compose a hexagram: 8 × 8 = 64, by composition rather than storage. Every edge is one assertion: click an edge or a name to read it in the panel. Ungroup separates the bundles into their individual edges; isolate keeps only the selected name's edges, drawn separately; zoom to selection spreads a selection until its edges are individually clickable. The Luoshu web sets the nine positions in the hierarchy with their edges to the trigrams placed on them and to their phases — the centre position has no trigram, and draws no such edge. The Hexagrams view changes the geometry itself: rings by derivation, phases at the core, the eight trigrams in the next ring, and the sixty-four hexagrams on the rim, each inside its upper trigram's sector — sector membership is the upper trigram, a short radial tie runs from that trigram into it, and one edge runs from its lower trigram — the foundation, read first — into it. Composed shows the sixty-four as single six-line units; Decomposed splits each glyph into its two stacked trigrams and lights both parent ties, folding the sixty-four back into the eight.

4 · How the hexagrams are composed

Eight nodes only: the trigrams. Each ribbon joins a lower trigram to an upper trigram — a hexagram is built and read from the bottom up, the lower trigram forming its bottom three lines and the upper trigram its top three — and there is exactly one hexagram for every ordered pair — the ribbon is that hexagram, numbered and named in the King Wen sequence. Ribbons that loop back to their own node are the eight pure hexagrams, one trigram doubled. Click a ribbon to see the hexagram it is, drawn as its six lines; click a trigram for the sixteen hexagrams it takes part in — eight as upper, eight as lower. The sixty-four are generated here by the 8 × 8 composition, not listed as an inventory.

5 · The Wheel — yin–yang at the centre, the five phases in a ring

At the centre, yin and yang divided by the diameter: yang above the line, yin below it. Around it, a ring of the five phases in generating order — five sectors of 72°, laid clockwise from the left-hand end of the diameter in the Chinese orientation (south at the top, east at the left): Wood begins at the left balance point and Fire crosses the top, both inside the yang half; Earth is centred on the right-hand end of the diameter and split evenly by it, half in yang and half in yin; then Metal and Water in the yin half, Water closing the ring at the edge of yin, back at the balance point where Wood begins. Each sector names its season and carries its associations — direction, colour, organ, flavour, tone and Luoshu number — inside its own ring-sector. This Earth is the seasonal Earth — 長夏， late summer, the pivot between Fire and Metal — not the centre placement other schemes give it: the centre here belongs to the yin–yang division, and Earth's Luoshu number 5 rides in its sector. The bagua is not drawn on this wheel: the eight trigram stations (45° apart) and these five sectors (72° apart) partition the circle differently, so seating the trigrams over the sectors made the drawing misstate their elements — pure-yin Kūn, for example, fell inside the Fire sector. The trigrams stand alone in their own chart (the Bagua tab), where each element is stated as an association rather than implied by position. Different charts show different aspects of the same phenomena — the Luoshu presentation gives the centre to Earth (5); this one gives it to yin–yang.

6 · The Bagua — the eight trigrams standing alone

The eight trigrams in their own traditional frame — an octagon — around a yin–yang centre, with no Wuxing ring beneath them. Each trigram stands at its Later Heaven station: Lí south at the top, Zhèn east at the left, Kǎn north at the bottom, Duì west at the right, and the four corner trigrams between. A trigram's element is carried explicitly, as a written association under its name — Qián and Duì are Metal, Zhèn and Xùn are Wood, Lí is Fire, Kǎn is Water, Kūn and Gèn are Earth — never implied by whatever sector might lie beneath it, because there is no sector beneath it. That separation is the point of this chart: the correspondence is a table keyed by direction, and the two partitions of the circle (eight stations of 45°, five sectors of 72°) cannot share one geometry without the drawing starting to misstate it. Earth in the bagua is itself the clearest case — it occupies no arc at all, but two opposed corner stations, Kūn at the southwest and Gèn at the northeast, the diagonal pair where the cycle turns. Each glyph is vector lines drawn from its own line data — Kūn's three broken lines read as pure yin at any size — and Legge's account of each trigram's symbolism is in the inspector.
